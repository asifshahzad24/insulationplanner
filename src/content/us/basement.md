## How the basement wall calculation works

### 1. Measure the walls

Choose **Measure walls** and enter the basement's perimeter and wall height, plus the number of doors and windows. The calculator deducts 21 sq ft per door and 12 sq ft per window. Set doors to 0 if the basement has none, and measure small basement windows yourself if you'd rather be exact.

Insulate the full height of the wall, from the floor slab to the top of the concrete. The rim joist above it needs its own treatment (see below).

### 2. Find the target

| Climate zone | Basement wall target |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | None published |
| 3–4 (except 4C) | R-13 |
| 4C, 5–8 | R-19 |

These are ENERGY STAR's recommended levels, which use the 2021 IECC's cavity insulation option. The code also allows continuous foam on its own, or foam plus a framed wall; the [PNNL table of IECC requirements](https://basc.pnnl.gov/information/2009-2021-iecc-and-irc-minimum-insulation-requirements-new-homes) lists those alternatives.

### 3. Pick an approach

- **Foam board only:** sheets glued to the concrete. No cavity limit, so the calculator stacks layers until it reaches the target.
- **Framed wall with batts:** a stud wall in front of the concrete. The batts are limited by the stud depth, which defaults to a 2×4 (3.5 in).
- **Both:** foam on the concrete, then a framed wall in front of it.

## Worked example: a 30 × 40 ft basement in zone 5A

- Perimeter: 140 ft. Wall height: 8 ft. Gross area: 1,120 sq ft
- Minus 4 windows at 12 sq ft: **1,072 sq ft net**
- With 10% waste: **1,179 sq ft**
- Zone 5A target: **R-19**

| Approach | Layers | To buy | Cost | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XPS board | 2 in R-10 + 2 in R-10 | 74 sheets | $4,016–$4,342 | R-20 |
| Polyiso board | 2 in R-13 + 1 in R-6 | 74 sheets | $3,303 | R-19 |
| EPS board | 3 in R-11.7 + 3 in R-11.7 | 74 sheets | $2,589 | R-23 |
| 2×4 wall, fiberglass batts | R-15, 3.5 in | 18 bags | $840–$1,232 | R-15 |
| Closed-cell spray foam | 2.92 in | Installed by a pro | $4,700–$7,834 | R-19 |

The 2×4 wall with batts alone falls short, and the calculator warns that R-19 won't fit in the cavity.

### Foam plus a framed wall

To model both, run the calculator twice. First, 1 in of XPS (R-5) on the concrete: **37 sheets, $1,109**. Then set **What's there now** to "I know the R-value", enter 5, and choose fiberglass batts in a 2×4 wall: R-15 batts, **18 bags, $840–$1,232**. Together they reach **R-20**, and the foam sits where it does the most good, against the cold concrete.

In zone 4A the target drops to R-13. The same basement then needs 2 in plus 0.5 in of XPS (74 sheets, $2,858–$3,021), or a 2×4 wall with R-13 batts (10 bags, $610–$905).

## Moisture: why foam goes against the concrete

Concrete is cold and lets moisture through from the soil. If warm, humid indoor air reaches it, water condenses on the wall. Fiberglass batts pressed directly against concrete can trap that moisture and grow mold. Rigid foam bonded to the wall, with taped or sealed joints, keeps indoor air off the concrete and keeps the inside face of the wall warmer.

- **Fix water problems first.** Grading, gutters and downspouts, and cracks that leak. Don't insulate a wall that gets wet.
- **Seal the foam joints** with tape or foam sealant so air can't get behind it.
- **Avoid a second vapor barrier.** Don't add plastic sheeting on the room side of a wall that already has foam against the concrete unless your local code requires it, because moisture can get trapped between the two.

## Fire protection

Foam board isn't meant to be left exposed in a living space. Building codes generally require it to be covered with a fire-rated thermal barrier, most often ½ in drywall. Check with your building department, and follow the foam maker's instructions.

## Don't forget the rim joist

The rim joist sits on top of the foundation wall, where the house framing meets the outside, and it is easy to miss. Cut foam board to fit each bay, seal the edges with canned spray foam, or have it spray-foamed while a contractor is on site.
